Consistency appears in very different forms

Community accounts. In the Dutch preparation thread, one rider describes short commuting rides several days a week plus occasional 50 km outings. Another describes easier endurance skating and leg-strength work. The goals thread includes a participant who wanted more skate-specific preparation instead of relying mainly on running and cycling. These are individual reports with different starting points, rather than a prescribed weekly schedule.[2] [6]

Bad weather changes the available tools

Community accounts. The winter discussion includes cycling, rowing, weights, cross-country skiing, indoor trainers and balance practice. It also contains disagreement about training intensity and a caution about stationary push practice loading the back differently. The useful question is which part of preparation an alternative activity serves. These experiences do not show that it replaces skating hour for hour.[8]

Rehearse more than distance

Community accounts. Long rides also reveal equipment comfort, whether a drink or snack remains appealing and whether carrying a full hydration pack becomes uncomfortable. One Dutch first-timer’s detailed report links lower-back discomfort to the weight they carried and identifies chafing as a major problem. That sort of observation can be more actionable than a distance total alone.[6]

Keep a record you can use next time

Community accounts. The large 2023 debrief includes a suggestion to journal the experience before the next packing cycle. A useful record separates what was planned from what happened: riding time, stops, food, gear changes and things that failed. Use this as a way to organise your own experience, rather than as a training prescription.[7]

A daily streak records adherence, not a required programme

Community accounts. One frequent contributor progressed from day 259 of skating more than a mile daily to completing a full year of at least one mile a day. This is the continuation of one person’s habit. Their accompanying claim that it healed several health issues does not include diagnoses, measurements or an independent assessment.[4] [5]

Editorial conclusion. A useful training record can include consistency while still recording interruptions and recovery. Compare the streak with the contrasting injury accounts before treating daily riding as a rule. These experiences do not establish a minimum streak or an age-based preparation timetable.

Consistency reports are not a daily-volume prescription

Reported experience. One skater reported more than a mile every day during 2025, including 20 miles each day in May, and more than 2.22 miles daily in 2026. The account did not describe recovery, injuries or a resulting Ultra performance. A separate motivational reply warned that waiting for ideal temperature, dry weather or enthusiasm could turn days without skating into weeks or months.[1] [3]

Editorial conclusion. Use these as examples of commitment, not a requirement to maintain a streak or train in unsafe conditions. Record what a session helps you practise and how you recover, alongside the mileage.

Separate planned changes from evidence that they worked

Community accounts. After 167 miles at Dutch Ultra, one rider hoped to reach 200 with a proper LDP board and more skating practice, instead of relying mainly on running and cycling. They also intended to skip the 55-mile bike ride they had done two days before the previous event. A reply thought the board upgrade alone might be enough and preferred varied training with age. Neither view had a follow-up result in that discussion, so the relative effects of equipment, specific practice and pre-event activity remain unresolved.[2]

Make it practical

Editorial prompts based on the discussion above.

  • Record conditions and equipment alongside mileage.
  • Try the practical tasks you will need: bottles, layers, lights and food access.
  • Keep individual training examples separate from a programme tailored to you.
