Moving speed is not overall average speed

Community accounts. The goals discussion includes an ambitious 14 mph expectation followed by a 113-mile result, and an inline rider’s account of a fast early pact that unravelled. Both illustrate the difference between a speed that feels possible early and the distance achieved across the entire event. The calculator on this site makes the arithmetic explicit; it does not predict what your body can sustain.[1]

Continuous riding and planned breaks

Community accounts. One Dutch participant described riding blocks separated by 30-minute stops. A support person reported a 250-mile plan split into five 50-mile blocks, with three one-hour breaks and a two-hour night break. Another rider found restarting after stopping difficult. These approaches serve different people and goals; they do not establish an optimal stop schedule.[4] [6]

Editorial conclusion. Compare these approaches with your goals and how you feel after restarting.[4] [6]

A lower target can still be a successful first event

Community accounts. The first-timer discussion follows someone aiming for 100 miles, taking hours off overnight after food problems, and eventually reaching the target. Other goal-setting replies explicitly use three possible outcomes. This leaves room for adaptation without presenting persistence through illness or injury as a rule.[1] [5]

Rest needs a place and a plan

Community accounts. Pit-crew and family-logistics discussions describe lights, noise and difficulty sleeping at the venue. Accounts disagree about music schedules. Their common planning value is that a tent alone is not a guarantee of rest; contributors discuss eye masks, ear protection, sleeping surfaces and accommodation. Confirm what the current venue actually permits and provides.[2] [3]

The same venue produced different sleep reports

Reported experience. In one family discussion, a participant remembered the garages becoming quiet after music stopped after dark. Another remembered music throughout the race and said the nominal 10 pm quiet period before race day did not reliably mean quiet. Other replies singled out garage snoring. A later supporter thread variously recalled no night music, a stop around midnight, or music late into the night and early again. These accounts cannot establish a dependable quiet window.[2] [3]

Reported experience. Light was another problem: participants reported pit or parking lights staying on both the night before and race night. Their sleep arrangements included eye masks, earplugs with additional over-ear protection, a cot without a tent, and a camping mat or air mattress in a vehicle with flat seats. One camper preferred a hotel after finding track sleep difficult. A vehicle’s ability to block sound does not establish a safe temperature inside it.[2] [3]

Reported experience. A separate suggestion was a hammock between concrete pillars; permission and suitable attachment points were not established. The useful requirement is a rest arrangement you can actually use, including a way to lie down, rather than assuming any available chair or tent will provide sleep.[2]

Distinguish a paper schedule from a demonstrated result

Community accounts. The rider who finished 113 miles after expecting 14 mph for 24 hours returned with 125 miles as a first target and 150 if time allowed. They thought 200 might work as four 50-mile blocks with one-hour rests, but explicitly left feasibility to the day itself. That was a proposed schedule, unlike the completed block-based accounts above.[1]

Community accounts. The 15 mph inline account also contains an early warning: the rider noticed an unexpectedly high heart-rate response on the first lap and found the track surface unlike the Miami asphalt they knew. The group nevertheless held the pace for six hours before struggling; that rider quit below 150 miles, while one companion slowed to about 12 mph and exceeded 300. The different outcomes within one group are another reason not to borrow its opening speed as a target.[1]

Make it practical

Editorial prompts based on the discussion above.

  • Separate elapsed time, moving time and stop time in your plan.
  • Work through a conservative scenario as well as a best-case one.
  • Decide where rest would happen and who can help if the plan changes.
