Winter training and the limits of transfer

Indoor work can keep preparation moving, but different activities answer different questions.

What can I do when I cannot skate outside?

What people actually did

Reported experience. A cold-weather alternative reported in New Jersey was 10 minutes on an elliptical, stretching, 10 minutes brisk treadmill walking and another roughly 10 minutes stretching. The rider disliked gym sessions but used one when outdoor conditions were cold. Not a sufficient training prescription or measured transfer to skating.[6]

Reported experience. Winter alternatives included cycling for one hour at high heart rate and two-hour lower-intensity elliptical sessions every other day; the questioner later disclosed a knee injury and need for low-impact activity.[5]

Editorial conclusion. These are examples of sessions, not prescribed intensity or duration. Keep fitness work separate from proving that feet, equipment and supplies tolerate skating.

Injury, weather and crowded indoor space

Reported experience. One rider preparing after a calf strain planned a cycling century first and then a gradual rebuild of skating volume; another used running on a cold morning. No rehabilitation recommendation or proof of equivalent skate fitness.[8]

Reported experience. Unable to skate outdoors in winter, two riders used a local rink but found it crowded on New Year’s Day, completing 191 loops in 1 hour 56 minutes while navigating other users. No lap length; cannot convert to mileage or imply rink matches Ultra surface/traffic.[3]

Editorial conclusion. A rink may offer movement when roads are unusable, while adding its own traffic and turning constraints. Record the setting when comparing sessions.

How well does other training transfer?

Reported experience. After roughly nine miles mainly on a pump track, a rider explicitly wondered whether it helped or hindered Ultra preparation; no response resolving that transfer question is available. Paddling was still unavailable after the earlier shoulder problem. Do not assert pump-track mileage equals distance-skating preparation.[4]

Participant-reported figures. Annual-volume replies include a 2,500-mile aspiration, roughly 1,800 completed toward 2,100, and 500 miles over three months combining mountain biking and skating, followed by a hoped-for 500 in two months. Mixed-sport totals do not identify skating-specific volume. Targets are not required preparation volumes; one unit is inherited ambiguously from question.[7]

Editorial conclusion. A mixed-sport annual total is not a skating readiness test. Return to the practical questions to rehearse on the board when conditions allow.

Make the substitute match the available space

Community accounts. Just before the 2026 Miami event, a Virginia rider facing icy trails planned rowing-machine sessions and pumping in a garage. Another had used running and cycling because dry trails never coincided with time off work. These accounts explain why the substitutes were chosen; neither supplies a result showing that they replaced long outdoor rides.[1] [2]
