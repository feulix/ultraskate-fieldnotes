GPS, battery life and the difference between records

Your device log, a public live feed and the official lap count are different records.

Start-up and battery failures

Reported experience. A rider missed the first laps of the 2018 Ultra recording by starting before GPS acquisition, then found a Forerunner 935 lasted only 20 hours at the 2019 event. Another described a secondhand Vivoactive 3 failing after almost two years and a 100-mile training weekend. Battery mode, age and settings vary; do not use as current specifications.[6]

Opinion. Participants proposed reduced-sampling UltraTrac modes for longer battery life, with claimed 36–40-hour durations. These are dated user claims, not measured results in this discussion, and entail less frequent location sampling. Verify manufacturer documentation before describing current modes or runtimes.[6]

Editorial conclusion. A device lasting 20 hours is not enough evidence for a current product specification. Rehearse your own settings, charging and recording procedure before relying on them.

An empty live feed is not proof of a stop

Reported experience. A volunteer chart-maker reported that the previous year’s lap-timing website stopped uploading data for several hours; a spectator had mistaken the gap for the analyst sleeping. Live chart gaps can reflect upstream data failure rather than rider inactivity. No cause independently verified; do not infer race timing system failure from public upload failure.[4]

Open question. A live eight-hour update explicitly noted a bug in one rider’s data while projecting another toward 320 miles. A projection or interim leaderboard should not be treated as a final verified result. Screenshot unread; neither result independently verified.[5]

Editorial conclusion. Keep provisional projections separate from confirmed results. Do not explain a gap by inventing what a rider was doing.

Missing recordings do not mean missing effort

Reported experience. A rider failed to follow their route app and ended up hiking through woods with wet shoes, then pushed hard for 10 km while forgetting to resume Strava. They subsequently felt nauseated, drank little and developed leg cramps; the sequence is reported, not proof of one cause.[2]

Reported experience. A rider was reminded of an earlier 199.6-mile result when targeting 200; they said they would not repeat the mistake, but did not explain what caused the shortfall. Do not invent lap-counting or timing explanation.[3]

Editorial conclusion. Separate route navigation, recording controls and race scoring in your checklist. Official chip instructions belong to the organiser, not the GPS app.

Check the measurement behind historical totals

Community accounts. An inline participant said the official Miami lap length changed from 1.38 to 1.42 miles in 2025, while their own mapped lap measured 1.41. This made them uncertain how to compare a friend’s earlier result with a new target. These are reported figures, not independently checked course measurements. Before comparing editions, check the scoring distance used for each result rather than substituting an app’s route estimate.[1]
