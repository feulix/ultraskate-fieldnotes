Liquid versus solid is an unresolved preference

Community accounts. The pizza thread has riders describing a bad experience with too much Tailwind, others reporting good results on mostly liquid calories, and others preferring regular meals and snacks. No single menu emerges as a consensus. The recurring practical theme is to know how your chosen foods and drinks behave for you before event day.[7]

Variety is also about willingness to keep eating

Community accounts. In the Dutch preparation and vegetarian discussions, contributors mention flavour fatigue and wanting something salty or substantial after hours of sweet drinks. Examples include potatoes, rice, bananas, applesauce pouches, crisps and familiar snacks. These are options people reported using, not a nutritionally complete menu or evidence that a food will suit everyone.[1] [4]

Vegetarian and vegan accounts are not the same as one another

Community accounts. The vegetarian/vegan thread includes a long-term vegan participant who uses drink powders and snacks, a lifelong vegetarian who describes provided meals and fruit pouches, and others who mix ordinary foods with gels. Some examples elsewhere in the thread contain honey or dairy. Check the original contributor’s approach and the actual ingredients rather than treating every suggestion in that thread as vegan.[1]

Keep medical and numerical claims in context

Community accounts. Community advice sometimes includes personal experiences with diabetes, insulin management, caffeine and intake targets. An individual health plan needs advice suited to that person. See the boundary between experience and medical guidance before interpreting symptoms or medication advice.[5] [6] [7]

Resupply belongs in the food plan

Community accounts. One solo rider prepared 12–14 mixed bottles in rows by flavour, with caffeinated and non-caffeinated options plus plain water. They used about 8–10 because they also ate meals. Another shared a race-map spreadsheet but explicitly said the race did not follow it exactly. These accounts concern organisation, not an hourly intake prescription.[5]

Frequent fuel and proper meals can coexist

Community accounts. A later discussion opens with a preference for small, regular amounts and avoiding heavy meals. A reply describes a different successful arrangement: cereal and banana before the start, gels roughly every thirty minutes when remembered, catered lunch and dinner, then turkey sandwiches at midnight and breakfast. It was that rider’s first edition spent riding throughout the 24 hours. The account challenges a blanket rule to avoid meals; it does not establish a gel schedule for everyone.[3]

Community accounts. Another contributor preferred ordinary food to gels in both skating and cycling: sandwiches, canned food and fruit that could be eaten while moving. Strong flavours helped keep eating interesting, and they accepted a slower lap to eat rather than stopping.[3]

Community accounts. A 210-mile finisher instead alternated water with carbohydrate drink mixes for most calories, using solids mainly for morale. They kept one familiar snack they could usually face even when nauseated—Slim Jims in their case. Their claimed 16,000-kcal deficit has no calculation or measurement method in the thread and cannot be treated as an energy requirement.[3]

Editorial conclusion. The useful comparison is timing, portion size, portability and what remains appealing late in the day. The same discussion’s explanations of digestion and claims about pickles preventing cramps are not established by these experiences. Keep symptoms and treatment questions with the medical guidance, rather than treating a successful menu as proof of its proposed mechanism.[3]

The same dinner helped one rider and troubled another

Reported experience. A January 2026 discussion began with a rider saying that staying on the board was easier than staying fuelled. Another described nausea all afternoon and into the evening at their first Ultra. The thread then split over arroz con pollo: one rider advised avoiding it, while another said their nausea eased after eating it. The second rider explicitly wondered whether the cooler evening, rather than the food, explained the improvement.[2]

Reported experience. Elsewhere in the same discussion, a rider feeling they were crashing reported renewed energy after ordering cheese pizza and went on to their first podium. That is an individual sequence of events, not evidence that pizza produces a performance gain. Together, these accounts support keeping accepted alternatives available and recording timing, heat and appetite alongside the food itself.[2]

A dietary selection is still a detail to confirm

Reported experience. One participant said they found little or no vegan provision in the previous year despite selecting vegan, and did not want the meals they had paid for. They also reported that the next entry bundled meals into the price instead of offering them separately. This is one historical complaint, not a statement about the current menu or registration terms.[2]

Editorial conclusion. Confirm ingredients, serving times and the actual dietary option for your edition, then retain familiar food you can use if the provided meal does not work for you. The fact that a meal is included does not answer whether it fits your diet or remains appealing during the race.

Food anecdotes do not identify a cramp remedy

Reported experience. A rider credited bananas with avoiding cramps. Another reported cramping at 60 miles in an earlier Ultra, then eating dill pickles and drinking their juice, having no further cramps and reaching 154 miles. The account gives no quantities or full timeline and does not isolate food from changes in pace, rest, weather or other intake. Neither story establishes a preventive treatment.[2]

Editorial conclusion. Keep a food you enjoy separate from a claim that it treats a symptom. Use the health guidance when deciding how to respond to concerning symptoms.

Use the menu examples with their original context

Community accounts. The lifelong vegetarian inline skater was aiming to stay out as long as possible, rather than race for a placing. Their examples included 70–80-calorie puréed food pouches, bananas, concentrated sports drink and homemade bars, with honey sweets and chocolate-covered almonds or coffee beans for enjoyment. A participant reporting nine vegan Ultras instead described relying heavily on Skratch and Tailwind alongside applesauce pouches, pickles, chips and other snacks, with occasional protein shakes. Honey-containing examples are not vegan; neither account is a complete menu or an intake target.[1]

Community accounts. One rider disliked gels because they were sticky and created rubbish. They planned to try high-carbohydrate powders and hot rice packets around 2 am, while another found cucumber and carrot snacks comfortable to eat. The rice-and-powder plan was prospective, not a successful event report. If a familiar food needs heat or refrigeration, connect that choice to your confirmed cooking and storage arrangements.[1]

Community accounts. The thread also disagrees about restrictions. One powder-and-gel user proposed avoiding anything above 8 g of protein, while others described protein shakes, bars, nuts or avocado. The participants gave no evidence for a universal 8 g cutoff. Preserve that disagreement instead of turning one person’s preference into a rule for every rider.[1]

Plan for a different appetite late in the event

Community accounts. A mixed-food participant described switching to liquid Tailwind for roughly the final third when eating became difficult. The questioner had the opposite preference for something that felt solid and found repeated Maurten gels excessively sweet. Another participant wanted fresh or savory snacks after hours of sweet drink and felt that eating at midnight provided reassurance as well as energy. They described their effort as moderate and explicitly thought the experience might differ for riders exceeding 250 miles with less time to stop.[1]

Community accounts. Check what product you actually tested. When the questioner described Tailwind as chalky, a reply asked whether it was the recovery version rather than the endurance mix. The product in the bag was never confirmed. The useful next step is to check the label and preparation instructions, not assume that everyone using the same brand has tried the same drink.[1]

Community accounts. Overnight caffeine preferences also varied: one participant used cold coffee intermittently, while another delayed caffeine and later used caffeinated gels or drink mix. Neither gave a dose or evidence of sustained alertness. These are taste and timing accounts, not a substitute for the rest needed for a safe journey home.[1]

Make it practical

Editorial prompts based on the discussion above.

  • Keep notes on foods and drinks already tested while skating.
  • Plan how supplies reach you and who prepares them.
  • Include familiar alternatives when taste or appetite changes.
  • Keep personal medical advice and exact intake targets outside a copied community menu.
